# Class Project 

## Purpose

The purpose of this class project is to deepen your understanding of
computational topology, through either investigating a new topic in the field or
by applying your knowledge to a data set.  Through this project, you will
practice essential skills in mathematical synthesis, empirical data modeling,
technical presentation, oral communication, and self-reflection. This project
carries direct long-term relevance by bridging pure geometric/topological theory
with practical problem-solving, equipping you with tools and scholarly
communication practices that directly apply to careers in advanced research,
computer science, mathematics and data-driven industry fields

## Task

The class project is open-form. The course project will be an opportunity for
you to investigate a research topic in computational topology (broadly
interpreted). This can take the form of investigating an application of
topological (or geometric) techniques to understanding your data set of choice,
or studying a computational problem in topology/geometry. We offer two main
options:

* Option 1 (Theory Track): Select a topic or paper in computational topology not
  covered in class (from a peer-reviewed publication or a textbook) and
  present its theoretical foundations, core proofs/intuitions, and significance.
* Option 2 (Applied Track): Apply concepts from computational topology (e.g.,
  persistent homology, Vietoris-Rips/Alpha filtrations, Euler characteristic
  curves) to a real-world or synthetic dataset of your choice.

Required Deliverables:

* Presentation: In-class presentation communicating your main theoretical
  synthesis or data results to your peers.  Your group can select whether your
  presentaion is a full class period or a half period.
* One-Page Summary:
    - If Theory: A 1-page overview highlighting key definitions, main
      findings/theorems, and why the topic matters.
    - If Applied: A 1-page report summarizing dataset details, topological
      methodology, key results (e.g., persistence diagrams/barcodes), and domain
      interpretation.
* Individual Self-Assessment: A short reflective document answering: What were
  your specific individual contributions to the project?  What were the
  contributions of each group member?  What were the main "sticking points" or
  technical obstacles you encountered, and how did you navigate them? (Expected
  to be at most two pages).
* Peer Evaluations: Complete a peer evaluation for each of the class
  presentations (including your own!).  These will include:
    - One up: what went well, and (briefly) why.
    - One down: what could have been improved, and (briefly) why.

Note: If your project overlaps with other classes or your paid research hours,
please discuss with me. This is ok, but some line will need to be drawn to
differentiate this course project from your other projects. And, approval from
the other instructor / research adviser will need to be ensured.

### Common Pitfalls to Avoid

* **Exceeding the One-Page Summary Limit:** Condensing research into a single
  page requires prioritization; avoid trying to include every proof step or raw
  output.
* **Failing to Translate Math to Domain Context (Applied):** Remember to explain
  what a $0$-dimensional or $1$-dimensional feature physically/conceptually
  represents in your specific dataset.
* **Omiting Sticking Points (Self-Assessment):** Encountering obstacles is a
  normal part of computational and theoretical math. Let me know what was
  difficult and how you navigated it.
